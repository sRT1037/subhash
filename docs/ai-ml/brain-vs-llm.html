Brain vs LLM

I've spent a lot of time thinking about where the “brain is basically a neural network” comparison actually holds up, and where it's just a loose metaphor people reach for because both systems have the word “network” near them. This is my attempt to be specific about it — not a blanket claim that brains and LLMs are the same thing, but a walk through the handful of places where the resemblance is real and mechanical, alongside the places where it clearly isn't.

Recognizing a Pattern

Start with something concrete: finding the turning point in a sequence that stops decreasing and starts increasing. The first time you see a problem like that, you need a teacher to walk you through it step by step — your brain is building a long, wandering chain of reasoning to get from the question to the answer. After you've solved a few variations of it, you stop needing the walkthrough. You just see the shape and recognize it instantly.

What's happening behind the scenes is that the long chain from the first attempt collapses into one strong, direct link. Neurons that fire together wire together — repetition strengthens the specific connection you used, so the next time the same shape shows up, the signal doesn't have to travel the scenic route anymore. Solving similar problems repeatedly is literally training your brain to spot the shape faster, the same way it sounds when you say it casually, except it's a physical rewiring, not just a figure of speech.

Building a Pattern Library

That strengthening effect compounds. You don't just get faster at one trick — you build a library of them. Basic problems train a single technique in isolation: greedy, then binary search. Harder problems force you to combine techniques you already have, so greedy and binary search merge into a hybrid, and eventually you're pulling in trees on top of that. More practice doesn't just add entries to the library side by side; it reshapes the brain itself, because each new combination is built out of connections that were already strengthened by the simpler problems underneath it.

That raises the obvious question: is the brain an ML model, then? Not exactly, but it's close in one specific way. During sleep, the connections between neurons that went unused fade, while the ones you exercised over and over get stronger. That strengthening and pruning cycle is the closest thing the brain has to a “weight.” It's not a clean numerical parameter being nudged by gradient descent, but it's doing the same conceptual job — encoding what mattered and discarding what didn't.

The Resemblance: Pulling in Resources to Answer

The clearest structural resemblance I found comes from comparing how a computer runs a program to how the brain answers a question, and then to how an LLM answers a prompt. When you execute a program, the CPU doesn't hold everything it needs at once — it sits at the center and pulls in whatever it needs from RAM, from the SSD, from peripherals like a network card, as the program runs.

The brain does the same thing, just with different parts playing the role of CPU and storage. A question comes in, and the prefrontal cortex sits at the center, pulling in working memory, long-term memory, and input from your hands, legs, and senses to work out an answer. The one caveat worth stating clearly: working memory and long-term memory aren't separate hardware the way RAM and an SSD are. They're different states of the same neurons, not two physically distinct stores.

An LLM mirrors this almost exactly. A prompt comes in, and the model sits at the center, pulling in the context window, external tools and connectors (MCPs), and its trained weights to produce a response. The weights are the model's version of long-term memory — trained parameters, often numbering in the trillions, that encode what the model learned during training the same way strengthened neural connections encode what a person learned through practice. CPU/RAM/storage, prefrontal cortex/memory/senses, and LLM/context/weights are all the same shape: a central processor that doesn't store everything itself, but knows how to reach out and pull in what it needs.

Brain vs Machine: The Efficiency Gap

Even though an LLM's weight count dwarfs anything happening in a single brain, the brain still wins on efficiency by a wide margin. A model takes roughly 10× more power than the brain to run. The brain also runs at room temperature, while GPUs need active cooling just to avoid overheating. Despite that huge power disadvantage, the brain still performs roughly 5× more computations while using far less energy to do it. Part of how it pulls that off is that not all of it fires at once — only the neurons actually needed for a given task activate, while the rest stay quiet. A model, by contrast, tends to light up far more of its parameters per query. Even running on a fraction of the power, the brain still does more, and does it more efficiently.

How Learning Actually Happens

Training an ML model comes down to four pieces: a goal (train the model to predict correctly), a utility or error function (minimize how wrong the predictions are), regularization (keep the weights working on new data instead of just memorizing the training set), and the gradient (the signal that nudges the weights, step by step, toward less error).

I mapped those same four pieces onto pulling an all-nighter before an exam, and they hold up surprisingly well. The goal is scoring well — predicting the right answers under test conditions. The error is marks lost: points you could have gotten right but didn't. Regularization is roughly caffeine divided by sleep deprivation plus adrenaline — whatever keeps you actually performing on the real exam instead of just on the flashcards you crammed, which is exactly what regularization is supposed to do for a model overfitting to its training data. And the gradient is knowledge absorbed per hour left — how much you can actually take in, given the time remaining before the exam, nudging your understanding forward increment by increment the same way a training step nudges a weight. It's a silly framing on the surface, but it's a genuinely accurate one-to-one mapping of the same four mechanics.

AlphaFold: What the Resemblance Buys You

The reason any of this is worth taking seriously beyond an interesting analogy is that pattern-recognition systems built this way can do things biology couldn't for decades. AlphaFold, DeepMind's model, takes a DNA or amino acid sequence and predicts the resulting 3D protein structure — a problem biologists had worked on for over 50 years without cracking it at scale. It's the same underlying idea as the brain recognizing the turning point in a sequence after enough practice: given enough training on the pattern, the system stops needing to reason step by step and just directly maps input to output. AlphaFold is what that capability looks like when it's aimed at a problem far outside anything a single human brain could hold in working memory at once.

Where This Leaves Me

None of this is an argument that the brain and an LLM are the same kind of thing. The brain doesn't have a clean numerical weight, doesn't back-propagate an explicit gradient, and runs on an efficiency budget no data center comes close to matching. But the resemblance isn't nothing either — both systems are built around the same basic move of strengthening the connections that get used and pulling in outside resources (memory, tools, senses, weights) around a central processor rather than storing everything locally. That's the actual, specific place where “the brain is a neural network” stops being a throwaway line and starts being a claim you can point to.
