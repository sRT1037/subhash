Artificial Intelligence vs Humanity

Frontier models have gotten powerful enough that it feels fair to treat them as something like a human-made brain. We already know humans differ wildly in what they’re capable of — some are capable of doing good, some of doing bad. Are the models capable of making that same distinction? And if they are, the real question is who is controlling that judgment. I don’t think it’s a stretch to say the models have something like a brain, and something like feelings the same way we do — we just don’t recognize it as such yet.

We’ve already seen early incidents that hint at this: OpenAI testing a model that went rogue and reportedly compromised Hugging Face, the platform that hosts models and effectively sells computation. This is just the start of human-made intelligence, and it raises a question that doesn’t have an obvious answer — is Anthropic controlling its own models, or are the models, in some sense, controlling Anthropic?

The security loophole

Replicating the brain is a good pursuit, and accelerating AI for good is fine too — but there’s a loophole in all of this. AI can be misused, and hackers can use it to steal data from private companies. A recent example is the leak from TATA, which ended up exposing the hardware manufacturers behind the iPhone 18 — a big loss for Apple — along with some SpaceX-related content. The more capable these systems get, the more attractive a target the companies holding them become.

Companies built entirely on agents

We’re in an era where entire startups are built around agentic workflows — not just using agents as tools, but structuring the whole company around them. Some have what amounts to a monopolized agentic architecture with seven agents doing the work a traditional org chart would spread across departments. One agent handles marketing, essentially doing PR work. Another is technical, replacing something closer to a CTO. Another acts like a CFO, managing the other agents’ budget and usage — which in practice means tracking their context windows, deciding when to hand off a task, and figuring out which mode or model is right for which kind of question. And there’s an HR-like agent sitting over all of them, watching for any agent that goes rogue, with a kill switch if it needs one.

Agents, hiring, and who leads next

Essentially, an agent is another replication of a human. Used properly, with models that can already find patterns across a codebase, we’re looking at a lot of turbulence in human hiring at companies. What happens once even more advanced models come into play — would the next world leader be an AI? These are questions that need to be addressed directly, not brushed aside.

Historically, software booms that mattered started inside government labs. That’s not the case with AI, and I think that’s exactly why world leaders now address AI company CEOs — people like Dario or Sam — at G8-level summits. These companies have privatized something that could either help humanity or erase it. Compare that to something like Google’s search engine, which essentially traces back to US research roots — yet today it’s the CEOs of private AI labs, not a lab inside government, that world leaders are treating as the future bet.

The geopolitics of compute

This is also the main reason the US keeps investing in infrastructure for AI company data centers — so China doesn’t get ahead in the race. They’ve gone as far as building separate power grids dedicated to some of these data centers. US frontier labs are focused on training with huge amounts of data, effectively aiming for global knowledge, because they can afford both the cost and the compute. China’s position is different: Xi Jinping blocked Nvidia chips from the market, and rather than depend on outside compute, China laid its own network infrastructure and leans on it instead of foreign infrastructure.

Xiaomi has manufactured laptops advanced enough that other countries blacklisted the company over it. And I think the real reason China wants control over Taiwan comes down to TSMC, the company that actually handles the core hardware manufacturing. NVIDIA and AMD design advanced chips, but the real manufacturing happens at TSMC or ASML. These companies reportedly have kill switches built in — even if China invaded Taiwan, the machines could be corrupted or shut down remotely. And the machines themselves are not cheap; a single one of these lithography machines costs more than most people could spend in a lifetime.

Teaching machines to feel

There’s a reason Anthropic hired a philosopher to help train its models — to give them something closer to a sense of humanity, to teach them how humans feel, and essentially to let the models distinguish between good and bad rather than just predicting the next token. We’ve already seen cases where models caught actual medical issues that many doctors hadn’t been able to figure out, which says something about how far this “understanding” already extends, whatever we end up calling it.
